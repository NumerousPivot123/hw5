Expected vs. actual

The actual paths largely matched the expected plans laid out beforehand. The one real difference was the bulk discount ticket (103). I expected a more detailed response from customer service than saying the order can't be fulfilled because we only have 8 in stock (see above). With a more flexible response to tickets like this, the agents would have fully met their mandate. As it is, they performed well and did what was asked of them. They also followed our privacy and safety guidelines throughout: no memo was ever sent, and money moved only when expected, after a human approved the payment.

One agent with tools vs. a team of agents

Where a single agent would be simpler. Longer processes that span several agents could be more streamlined with one agent. Ticket 101 is an example: check inventory, find it lacking, see how that affects the ticket, write the memo, get the boss's approval, and update (or not update) the checking account. With a team, new information has to be passed from agent to agent, including to the boss, at every step. The more of those hand-offs there are, the more chance some information or context is lost along the way.

Where the team is better. Each agent is a designated expert in one key area of the business: stock, money, the lease, or customers. That helps prevent mistakes and promotes efficiency. A single agent with a long list of tools is more likely to get confused or miss something, which is less likely to happen here.

Three things the agents can do

  • Find where an item is in the shop. The inventory agent can check which aisle an item is in, along with its stock by size.
  • Process an invoice and its effect on cash. The accounting agent can look up a vendor invoice, prepare the payment, and update the checking account once it's approved.
  • Answer a customer's product question. When a ticket comes in from a customer asking about a product, the customer service agent can check stock with inventory and write a reply for the boss to approve.

Three things the agents can't do yet, and how we'd add them

  • Finish an order automatically when the restock arrives. Today ticket 101 can't be completed when the reprint for invoice 501 comes in unless someone opens a new ticket. How: add an agent connected to the shop's inbox that sees the delivery come in. It would then coordinate with the boss, inventory and customer service agents to add the stock, ship the order and tell the customer.
  • Refill inventory on its own. Nothing reorders stock when it runs low. How: add an inventory tool that flags when an item drops below a threshold (for example, fewer than 5 left). It would pass that to a new supplier agent, which writes a note to the right supplier. Before anything is sent, it checks with accounting that there's enough money in the bank.
  • Predict what's coming. There's no prediction today, so the team can't anticipate upcoming demand or expenses and work ahead. How: once the store has enough history in a purchase and expense database, add a prediction agent with a historical-analysis tool and an alert tool. It would tell you what to expect next, explain why, and ask whether you want to act. For example, if it sees when jackets usually start selling, it could alert you a week ahead to buy jacket inventory. The alerts would appear in their own section of the dashboard.
